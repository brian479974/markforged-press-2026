01
Iterate Better.

R&D: faster iterations, bolder designs

In R&D, waiting is the most expensive part. Every few weeks spent waiting on an outsourced low-volume part is a few fewer design rounds. Bring printing in-house and each iteration is measured in days, not weeks.

X7Material: Onyx (prints on FX10)

Larsen Motorsports: a steering wheel fitted to one driver, in 2 days instead of 8

Jet dragster racing · U.S.

A jet dragster runs at 280 mph, and its off-the-shelf butterfly steering wheel is built to standard hand dimensions, so driver Josette Roach could not reliably reach critical controls. The team printed a fitted wheel on an X7, with Onyx as the base and continuous carbon fiber laid along the load paths, cutting part lead time from 8 days to 2. In real-world testing it was stiffer than the aluminum original, which flexed under load. Simulation software predicted the loads up front, so the team did not need to print ten intermediate versions to find the failure point.

Garry Rogers Motorsport: a brake duct pattern in 24 hours instead of two weeks

Racing team · Australia

Australia's oldest motorsport team has no CNC machining, so low-volume complex parts used to be outsourced. Complex shapes suggested by the engineers' CFD software cost money and two weeks each time. With Markforged carbon fiber printing, a brake duct pattern for a fiberglass mold went from AUD 1,000 (about USD 670) and two weeks to AUD 75 (about USD 50) and 24 hours. Around half or more of what the team now prints is end-use, including door lock covers, gearbox housings, steering wheel handles and camera mounts.

Produce Better.

Production: tooling without the wait, and less downtime risk

What holds up a line is often not the equipment but the fixtures and tooling. Outsourcing means two or three weeks each time, and a spec change starts the clock again. Make fixtures in-house and a revision can be on the line the same day.

Volkswagen Autoeuropa: die lifters cut from 60 kg to 4 kg

Vehicle plant · Portugal

Volkswagen's Palmela plant in Portugal introduced 3D printing in 2014 and continuous carbon fiber printing in June 2024. The first success was in stamping: die lifters went from 60 kg to just 4, improving ergonomics, efficiency and sustainability. It has since spread to a Y-connector for pipes in the body shop, a chemically resistant high-tension ring for paint shop robots and a universal tray in logistics, ready to be transferred across the Volkswagen Group.

Service: replacement parts made close to where they are needed

The Markforged view is to turn service into a competitive advantage: make replacement and service parts where they are needed for faster repairs. That only works if designs and print settings stay in sync between sites, so every site prints the same part.

Dana: one set of designs and print settings, shared across seven countries

Drivetrain and power systems · Global

Dana, a mobility supplier with more than a century of history, runs Markforged printers in seven countries and uses the cloud-based Eiger platform to share CAD designs, print settings and production data between engineering hubs, so each site prints tools and parts where they are needed. Viewpoint

AI is improving how cars are designed and built. What does Markforged industrial additive manufacturing give customers?

AI has made automotive design faster, but your factory has not necessarily kept up. Design software can propose several new shapes overnight, yet turning one of them into a physical prototype, fixture or mold still means an outside supplier and a two- or three-week wait at many plants. Every step faster on the design side adds another round of waiting.

Garry Rogers Motorsport shows the gap clearly. Australia's oldest racing team has no CNC. When its engineers reshaped a brake duct based on CFD simulation, every change meant outsourcing a new fiberglass mold pattern: AUD 1,000 and two weeks. Printed in carbon fiber on Markforged, the same pattern took 24 hours and AUD 75. For the first time, the shop could make parts as fast as the simulation could suggest them.

Speed is only the first advantage. Automotive plants need fixtures and parts that go on the line, not models to look at. Continuous fiber reinforcement is Markforged's patented technology: carbon fiber is laid continuously along the load path, so the strength is built into the part. FX10 puts composites and metal in one machine; with the Metal Kit it also prints 17-4PH and 316L stainless steel and H13 tool steel, right on the factory floor. Draw it in CAD, print it overnight, use it the next day.

The second advantage is data. For AI to improve production, physical parts have to become data as well. Markforged Simulation validates part performance and optimizes print settings before printing; FX10 scans the part with a laser micrometer as it prints, and Inspection verifies the dimensions, so every part comes off the printer with a measurement report. Each step from design to validation to measurement leaves data that can feed the next design round.

The third advantage is replication. Eiger is a cloud platform that keeps design files, print settings and the part library in one place. Dana shares the same designs and parameters across sites in seven countries and prints fixtures and parts locally when they are needed. A method proven in one plant can go straight to another without starting over.

If your lines in Taiwan, China, Hong Kong or Vietnam are still waiting on outsourced fixtures, start with one part. The test is simple: it goes on the line the next day, and the measurements match the drawing.
